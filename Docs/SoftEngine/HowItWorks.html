SoftEngine: how it works

The soft body engine in Assets/1_Scripts/PhysicsScripts/2_SoftEngine, explained from the body to the frame. State of 16 November 2026.

This document explains what the engine does and why it is built the way it is. It follows one physics step from start to end, then compares the result with the engine it replaces (1_PhysicsSolver), lists what was tried and thrown away, and ends with a reference of every setting. Numbers are measured on your M1 unless stated otherwise.

1. The idea in one page

Every body is a block of jelly cubes (a lattice) with the mesh you see glued inside it. Only the corners of the cubes, the nodes, ever move. Everything else, the visible mesh and the collision surface, is carried along by them.

A physics step is a few identical substeps (4 by default). Each substep does six things in a fixed order:

Three ideas carry the whole design:

  1. Positions first, velocity last. Every stage moves node positions. The velocity of a node is simply how far it really got in the substep, divided by the time. Nothing can "explode" from a force that is too large, because there are no forces, only places to be.
  2. Collision is planes between small convex pieces. The surface of every body is cut into small convex pieces. For every pair of pieces that could touch, the engine finds one plane that separates them at the start of the substep. If both pieces are still on their own side of that plane at the end, they never touched in between. That is the whole no-penetration argument, and it is checked on the real points, at the end, every substep.
  3. Two layers of contact. A fast, parallel, "good enough" solve pushes bodies apart and gives believable behaviour. A small, strict step after it (the limit step) is the guarantee. The first is allowed to be imperfect because the second cannot be fooled.
Where the code is. Core/SoftSolver.cs holds all data and the order of the stages. The jobs are in MotionJobs.cs (predict, elastic, velocity, damping, sleeping, visuals), DetectionJobs.cs (stage 3) and ContactJobs.cs (stages 4 and 5). LatticeBuilder.cs turns a mesh into a body. SoftWorld and SoftBody are the two components you put in a scene. Every job starts with a long comment that says what it does and why.

2. What a body is made of

When a SoftBody starts, LatticeBuilder lays a grid of cubes with 0.25 m edges over its mesh and keeps every cube the mesh reaches into. Those cubes are the cells, their corners are the nodes. The mass of the body is spread over the nodes.

Each cell is split into 5 tetrahedra: one in the middle and one in each of four corners. The elastic solve works on those. Every second cell uses the mirrored split, so the diagonal on a face shared by two cells is the same line for both and the surface has no cracks.

Every vertex of the mesh is glued into one tetrahedron: its position is a fixed mix of that tetrahedron's 4 nodes. Move the nodes and the vertex follows. The visible mesh and the collision surface are both glued in this way.

Pieces and hull points: what collides

For collision, the part of the surface inside one cell is cut into pieces, patches that are convex (they bend outwards only). A cell on a flat or rounded part of the surface has one piece. A cell where the surface bends inwards has several.

A piece is not stored as triangles. It keeps only a handful of its vertices, the hull points: just enough of them that no vertex of the patch sticks out of the shape they span by more than 1.25 mm. On your scene that is about 10 points per piece. At runtime the engine never looks at a triangle, only at these points. This is the single biggest reason collision is cheap: the question "how far apart are these two pieces" becomes a question about two small clouds of points.

Two details that took a failed attempt each to get right:

  • Hull points are picked per tetrahedron, not per cell. A tetrahedron deforms as one flat-sided unit, so points that span the patch at rest still span it when crushed. Picked per cell, vertices popped out of the hull under a hard crush and the real surfaces came within 3 mm.
  • The pieces of each block of 2 x 2 x 2 cells are stored next to each other as a chunk. That is what makes the search in section 5 fast.

3. One substep, start to end

SoftSolver.Substep runs the stages one after the other. Each stage is finished before the next starts. The stages that go over many things of the same kind are spread over all cores; the few that only walk through a list once run on the main thread directly. (I measured the alternative, chaining all jobs and waiting once at the end. It was not faster, and finishing each stage lets the solver skip whole stages when it sees they have nothing to do.)

After the substeps, once per step: SleepJob decides who is asleep (section 8), and SoftWorld draws the meshes.

Why 4 substeps and not 10. The old engine used 10. Substeps are the most expensive knob there is, since everything runs once per substep. Collision no longer needs small steps to be safe (the limit step is exact for any step length), so the number is set by how stiff you want materials to feel, and 4 is enough for that with the new elastic solve.

4. The elastic solve

Each tetrahedron compares its shape now with its shape at rest. That comparison is a 3 x 3 matrix F, the deformation gradient: the identity when the tetrahedron is at rest, a pure rotation when it has only turned. Two numbers are taken from it:

I = sum of the squares of F's 9 entries (3 at rest: how stretched it is overall) J = determinant of F (1 at rest: its volume, negative when turned inside out)

The material is "stable neo-Hookean" (Smith et al. 2018), the same energy the film and games industry uses for flesh and rubber. Its two stiffness numbers, mu (resists change of shape) and lambda (resists change of volume), come from the Young's modulus and Poisson ratio you set on the body.

The part that was wrong before, and the fix

To use that energy in a position-based solver it has to be written as "constraints", quantities that should be zero. The way you split the energy into constraints decides everything.

SplitConstraintsWhat happens
Textbook (Macklin, Mueller 2021)|F| and J - 1 - mu/lambdaNeither is zero at rest: one wants to shrink the tetrahedron to a point, the other to blow it up, and they only cancel. With one pass per substep and stiff bodies they do not cancel well enough. The body shakes itself apart.
Old engine, and my first version|F| - sqrt(3) and J - 1Calm, both zero at rest. But near rest both measure the same thing, volume. A sheared tetrahedron feels almost nothing, so the body has no real resistance to shear. That is why you needed values like 1e+08 to get something that felt stiff, and why floating bodies wobbled.
NowCD = sqrt(I + J*J - 4J) and CH = J - 1The same energy, grouped so both parts are zero at rest and measure different things: CD shape and size (stiffness mu), CH volume (stiffness lambda). To first order this is exactly Hooke's law with your mu and lambda. Stiffness values now mean what they say.

Both constraints pull on the same 4 nodes, so they are solved together as one small 2 x 2 system per tetrahedron. Solved one after the other, each would undo part of the other. The arithmetic is in double precision: at rest CD is the root of a difference of large numbers, and in single precision the rounding alone was enough to make a stiff body buzz.

Last, a stretch limit: no edge of a cell gets longer than maxStretch times its rest length (2 by default). A very soft body hit hard would otherwise be pulled into long strings before its own stiffness reacts.

What "stiff" can mean here. Each substep makes one pass over the tetrahedra. A light body with a very high Young's modulus is stiffer than one pass can express, and ends up as stiff as the pass count allows, not as stiff as the number says. More substeps make it stiffer. This is a property of every solver of this family, not a bug.

5. Finding contacts

Detection answers one question for every pair of pieces from different bodies: can these two get within skin (25 mm) of each other during this substep? If yes, it produces a plane to keep them apart. It works in three steps that each throw away most of what is left.

Broad phase: boxes inside boxes

Every piece gets a box. Boxes of the pieces of one chunk are wrapped in a chunk box, and the chunk boxes of one body in a body box. The search goes top down:

  • two bodies whose boxes do not overlap are done;
  • of the other body, only the chunks that reach into this body's box are looked at;
  • only for two overlapping chunks are the pieces compared one by one.

Most of the surface of most bodies is nowhere near another body and is dropped a whole chunk or a whole body at a time. Each body searches for its pairs on its own core.

The list is not rebuilt every substep. Each piece gets a "frame box": the box it needs now, stretched along where its nodes are heading for the next whole frame, plus some slack. The pair list made from frame boxes stays valid for as long as every piece stays inside its frame box, which PieceJob checks each substep. A pile lying still keeps its list for seconds. A falling body gets a new one about once per frame.

Skipping far pairs with one subtraction

The list still holds many pairs that are nowhere near touching (about 14 pairs for every real contact in your scene). When a pair was last examined and found far apart, it remembers a budget: the distance found then, plus how far both pieces had travelled by then. Every piece keeps a running total of how far it has travelled. As long as

budget - (travel + reach of A) - (travel + reach of B) > skin

the two are provably still more than skin apart at the end of this substep, whatever they did in between. That is one subtraction per pair, and it removes most of the list before any geometry is done.

Narrow phase: the plane between two pieces

For a pair that is close, PlaneJob looks for the direction n that keeps all of A's hull points as far as possible above all of B's. The distance along n between A's lowest point and B's highest is the slab. Because pieces are convex, the best slab is exactly the distance between them.

The search is GJK, the standard algorithm for the distance between two convex shapes. It starts from the n of the substep before, which is nearly right, so it usually needs two or three steps.

A pair gets a contact only if, given where the nodes are heading, the two could end closer than the contact solve or the limit step care about. Most contacts are marked idle: they are in the list so the limit step can check them, but there is nothing to push.

The key fact. The plane is found at the positions at the start of the substep and is fixed for the substep. Nodes move in straight lines within a substep. So if the points of A are above the points of B along n at the start, and still are at the end, they were above all the way. No sweeping, no time of impact, no tunnelling at any speed. This is why a ball at 140 m/s does not pass through the floor.

6. Solving contacts

Every contact has a target: how far apart, along its plane, the two pieces must end.

  • Pieces further apart than skin may come as close as skin, no closer.
  • Pieces already closer than skin are held where they are and moved back out a little each substep (at most pushOutSpeed). A fixed shove every substep would keep kicking a body that just lies there, so the push out slows down as it nears skin.

One body per core

The usual way to solve contacts is one loop that visits them one after the other, moving both bodies of each. That loop cannot be split over cores, because two contacts may move the same node.

Here a sweep gives every body its own core. A body moves only its own nodes. What it needs to know about the other side it reads from a snapshot of where every node was when the sweep began. Both bodies of a contact therefore see the same shortfall, and each takes its part of it: the lighter side more, the heavier less. Together that is exactly what was missing.

Inside one body, the contacts are still visited one after the other, and each first looks at how far its spot has already been moved in this sweep by its neighbours, so contacts that want the same thing do not add up.

Three sweeps run per substep. This gave about half the solve time of the old single loop, and it scales with the number of bodies.

Where a contact pushes: spots, and moving cells whole

A normal contact pushes at one spot per side, the middle of where the two pieces face each other. A spot is a fixed place in its cell and moves with the cell's 8 nodes. A push moves every node by its share of the spot, so the cell keeps its shape and the elastic solve has nothing to undo in the next substep. (Splitting a push by the mass of each node is what a real point force would do, but outer nodes are lighter than inner ones, the cell bends a little with every push, and a stiff body lying still keeps buzzing.)

A pair heading for the hard limit becomes firm: it is measured on the real hull points instead of one spot, and each side answers by moving its whole cell. Without this, one spot misses corners that come in fast or at an angle, the limit step has to stop them, and the limit step holds back the heavy side as much as the light one. A 1000 kg cube catching a 1 kg ball in the air was slowed from 17.8 to 8 m/s before firm contacts existed.

Floor and friction

Floor contacts work per hull point. Every point below its target asks its nodes for the lift that brings it back, and every node takes the largest lift asked of it. That satisfies all floor contacts at once and does not depend on their order, so a body that lands straight is not pushed sideways.

Friction runs in the last sweep, when it is known how hard each contact pushed in total. The sideways slide of the two spots against each other is taken back, at most friction x push (Coulomb's law), and each side again takes its share of the slide as it was when the sweep began.

The price of going parallel. Because each body acts on its own, a contact does not hand exactly the same momentum to both sides. In a head-on test of two equal balls about 8% of the momentum goes missing. You said that does not matter to you; it is the trade that bought the speed.

7. The limit step: the guarantee

The contact solve is fast and usually leaves nothing to do. But it runs a fixed three sweeps and is allowed to be wrong. LimitJob is the last thing that moves nodes in a substep, and it is the part that cannot be wrong.

For every contact it measures the real hull points where the nodes are now, in double precision:

(lowest point of A along n) - (highest point of B along n) >= limit limit = the smaller of: the slab at the start, hardSkin (12.5 mm)

If that holds, the pieces were apart at the start, are apart at the end, and moved in straight lines in between, so they were apart all the way. Because the plane is the best one at the start of every substep, the distance between two pieces can never shrink below hardSkin. The real surface lies inside the hulls (at most 1.25 mm outside them per side), so surfaces cannot touch.

A contact that fails is cut: the room that was available is split between the two sides by how much each came closer, and every hull point that went past its side's share is brought back. How it is brought back depends on how often the list has already been gone through:

  • Slide is gentle: the node stays free to move along the plane and away from it, so nothing sticks to what it touches. This replaced the older behaviour (always "stop"), which was the likely cause of the soft sphere hanging on the cube edge.
  • Stop and freeze only ever shorten a node's path, and a frozen contact holds whatever else happens, so the loop always ends. What is left has passed the very check that defines "safe", with the positions that are stored.

Taking motion away for one contact can break another that was fine, so the list is checked again until one full pass finds nothing. After the first pass only the contacts of bodies that had a cut are checked again. In a calm scene there are no cuts at all and this stage costs 0.04 ms. Nothing here can add energy: a node only ever moves less than it wanted to.

The second look

Detection assumed that contacts push a node at most regionMargin (25 mm) away from where the elastic solve left it. Something fast and heavy hitting something light breaks that assumption: the light body is thrown much further and may now reach pieces it was never paired with. So the last contact sweep reports which bodies it pushed further than that. Only for those, LatePieceJob finds the pieces concerned and detection runs a second time just for them, before the limit step. Usually no body is reported and none of this runs.

8. Damping, sleeping, the step-time limit

Damping (per body)

An elastic body with no damping wobbles forever, like a perfect spring. A free E = 67 ball was still wobbling after 10 seconds. DampJob runs right after the velocities are known. It works out how the body is moving and turning as a whole (the motion with the same momentum and angular momentum the nodes have now) and takes away a part of everything else. So a damped body falls, flies and spins exactly like an undamped one and rests in the same shape. It only stops wobbling. The setting is a time: after damping seconds about a third of a wobble is left. 0.1 is the default.

Damping is also your bounce control: bounce here comes from the body deforming and springing back, and damping decides how much of that survives. Lower damping, more bounce.

Sleeping

A body remembers where its nodes were. As long as they stay, on average, within sleepDistance (1 cm) of there, the body is going nowhere, however much it trembles. After sleepTime (0.5 s) of that it falls asleep: the solver treats it as a static body, which costs almost nothing.

A body that does get further "went somewhere". It remembers the new place, starts counting again, and wakes every sleeper whose box touches the box of where it was, where it is, and where it is heading. So whatever is about to hit a sleeping body wakes it, and so does a support that leaves.

Sleeping is also the cure for the twitching of soft bodies trapped under heavy ones. That twitch is not a vibration. The body is squeezed between two things that both push, and every substep the contact solve shuffles its nodes a few millimetres. I measured three damping schemes against it and none helped, because it is positions being shuffled, not velocities. A body that is going nowhere falls asleep and the shuffle stops completely. In your scene with damping 0.1, 38 of 39 moving bodies were asleep after about 10 seconds and the step cost 0.85 ms.

The step-time limit

With maxStepMs set, a step that would take longer protects the frame in two stages:

  1. Hurry. A substep that takes more than its share makes the next ones save time on contacts: one sweep, and the limit step stops and freezes at once instead of sliding first. Nothing gets through either way, but hard-squeezed bodies stick together while it lasts. This is aimed at where the time actually goes: in crush scenes the limit step alone was up to 29 of 45 ms.
  2. Slow motion. If the next substep would not fit, the step ends early. The substeps that were done are as long and as careful as always, so the simulation simply advances less than a full frame. One substep is always done.

With a 6 ms limit the worst steps of the three crush tests went from 23, 45 and 15 ms to about 6 ms, at roughly 90% speed.

Repeatable

A limit that reads the real clock makes every run different, because the clock never says the same thing twice. With repeatable on (the default), the limit instead counts the work a substep did: its cells, pairs, contacts and cuts, each times a cost measured once on your machine. The same work gives the same number, so two runs are identical to the last digit. The estimate matched real times within about 10% on average. The trade: on a slower machine the frame takes longer instead of the simulation slowing more, because nothing looks at the clock. Turn it off to have the limit follow the real frame time exactly.

10. What was tried and dropped

Most of the design is what was left after something simpler failed. These are worth knowing so nobody tries them again without a new idea.

TriedWhy it was dropped
Hashed grid broad phase0.7 ms per rebuild on the pile: 17,000 bucket lookups for 2,500 pairs. Boxes inside boxes are several times cheaper for bodies that are mostly not touching.
Rebuilding the pair list only for pieces that left their boxPieces leave one by one, so the fixed cost was paid every substep. Worse than full rebuilds.
Signed distance fieldsPoint samples against a distance field cannot promise zero penetration with a 12.5 mm skin, and the field of a deformed cell is not its rest field.
Hull points picked per cellUnder a hard crush vertices popped out of the hull. Real distance fell to 3 mm.
Clamping how far a contact may pushBrakes heavy fast bodies and permanently scrambled the 140 m/s ball.

Firm contacts that push single hull pointsEach side acting alone added energy (a cube bounced to 11 m). Firm contacts must move the whole cell.
Giving back over-pushes in the parallel solveWorks in a single loop. With two bodies doing it at once the result depended on whether the sweep count was odd or even, and a stack buzzed at up to 1 m/s.
Friction that removes "its share of the current slide" per contactContacts share nodes, so each body ended up removing the whole slide. The slide reversed every substep and grew.
Damping inside the elastic constraintsWith one pass per substep it softens stiff bodies: a ball rested 10 cm lower. Solving the damper separately exploded.
Damping per cellOnly smooths velocity between neighbours. A bounce, which is the whole body deforming, was untouched.
Fewer, larger jobsNo gain. The time is real work, not scheduling.

12. Known limits

  • Play mode was verified by you, not by me. Everything was developed against the headless test runner. The drawing code in SoftWorld in particular has only ever run on your side.
  • Momentum is not exactly conserved between two moving bodies (about 8% lost in the head-on test). Energy is never gained.
  • No separate restitution or static friction. Bounce is damping and stiffness; friction is one number.
  • Soft bodies pinched under heavy ones tremble until they sleep. Sleeping hides it; the cause (the contact solve shuffling a body that has nowhere to go) is still there.
  • A body crushed far beyond what it can bear can stay tangled. The test is a 1000 kg cube at 80 m/s onto a 2 kg ball.
  • Bodies created overlapping stay overlapping. Planes between convex pieces can keep things apart, they cannot untangle them.
  • One very heavy substep can still exceed Max Step Ms, since one substep is always done.
  • The narrow phase is the largest remaining collision cost (0.44 of 1.43 ms): the distance search runs every substep for every pair within about 6 cm. Reusing a plane for the substeps of one frame would keep the guarantee and is the next thing to try.
  • Sleeping bodies still have their hull points recomputed every substep. Skipping that would bring a fully sleeping scene well under the 0.85 ms measured.
  • Not built: collision with Unity colliders and rigidbodies, removing a body at runtime, forces, grabbing, raycasts, collision events, and a standalone build. You chose to leave these for later.
